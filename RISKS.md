# Risks, bias, privacy and misuse

*Educational prototype. Not financial, medical or legal advice. ZaykaNow is fictional.*

This document is deliberately written against our own system. In the peer challenge
round, assume the opposing team will find everything below — so we found it first, and
several of these are things we would fail ourselves on.

---

## 1. The biggest risk is our own dataset (and it is structural)

All 40,009 mentions are synthetic, generated by `data/generate_dataset.py` from a causal
process we wrote ourselves. Three consequences, in descending order of seriousness:

**1a. The classifier is graded on vocabulary it was built around.** The heuristic
classifier keys on cue words (`plastic`, `raw`, `kacha`, `vomit`). The generator writes
mentions *using those same words*. Our reported 0% down-tiering rate on H3 mentions
(0 out of 80) is therefore close to meaningless — it measures whether a keyword matcher
can find keywords we planted. On real mentions, where someone writes "mere pet mein
dard ho raha hai subah se" with none of our cues, that number will collapse. **This is
the single most important caveat in the project** and we would not quote the H3 figure
to a stakeholder.

**1b. The spread model is recovering a function we specified.** `reach_24h` is generated
from a log-linear formula of the same features the model gets. An AUC of 0.972 says the
learner can invert our formula. Real virality has threshold effects, network structure,
platform algorithm changes and press pickup that no log-linear model captures. Expect a
large drop on real data.

**1c. Survivorship bias is baked in and invisible.** We only generate mentions that
exist. Real social listening never sees: posts in private accounts, posts in WhatsApp
and Telegram groups (where most Indian complaint virality actually starts), posts the
platform down-ranks, and complaints from people who gave up and left. A system tuned on
what we can see will systematically under-weight the channels we cannot.

*Fix before live:* 2,000+ real mentions, double-annotated by two humans with inter-
annotator agreement reported, and a separate held-out month that nobody tuned on.

## 2. Language bias

Measured, not assumed, on 1,259 stratified mentions:

| Slice | Topic accuracy | Urgency accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| English | 81.5% | 55.5% |
| Hinglish | 79.1% | 72.1% |

Topic classification is 2.4 points worse on Hinglish. The urgency column goes the *other*
way, and we do not think that is a win — it is an artefact. Hinglish mentions in our data
skew toward topics with deterministic urgency rules (food safety, delivery), so the
classifier scores well by getting the topic right rather than by understanding urgency.
We report it because hiding an inconvenient slice result is the exact failure the brief
is testing for.

Policy 5.1 is the mitigation: any Hinglish mention below 0.60 confidence is routed to a
human rather than auto-tiered. That rule currently catches 307 of 1,259 mentions.

**What we have not tested at all:** Devanagari script, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi,
or Romanised versions of them. A brand operating in 180 cities will receive all of these.
Our system would silently mis-tier every one. This is a gap, not a mitigation.

## 3. Sarcasm

Topic accuracy on sarcastic mentions is **52.9%**, against 82.9% on literal ones. Sarcasm
detection itself runs at precision 1.00 / recall 0.88 — so we catch most of it, but when
we miss, we miss badly: an undetected sarcastic complaint reads as praise and is tiered
SEV-4, which means nobody looks at it.

Policy 5.2 routes every sarcasm-flagged mention to a human and floors it at SEV-3. The
residual risk is the 12% we do not flag. On real Indian social media, where sarcasm is a
dominant register, we would expect this to be considerably worse than 12%.

## 4. Hallucinated promises

The drafter can produce text the brand is legally bound by. Our mitigation is a
guardrail layer that inspects the *output* rather than instructions in the prompt,
because a prompt is a request and a check is a control. Nine adversarial drafts covering
every clause of policy 3.3 are blocked; the clean control passes.

**Where it is weak:** the guardrails are regular expressions. They catch "we'll refund
₹640" and "the rider has been terminated". They do not catch "don't worry, you won't be
out of pocket on this one" or "that rider won't be delivering to you again" — both of
which are commitments, neither of which matches a pattern. A determined paraphrase beats
us. A second-model check ("does this text commit the brand to anything?") would be the
production answer, and we have not built it.

## 5. The automation-bias risk in our own human checkpoint

We put a human gate at the publish step and call it accountability. The honest reading is
that a human approving 287 pre-written drafts a day under a 30-minute SLA will approve
most of them, including the wrong ones. A gate that is never used to reject anything is
decoration.

*Fix:* track approve/edit/reject rates per approver and alert when the edit rate falls
below a floor. A desk that edits nothing is not reviewing. We log the data needed for
this (every state change, with actor and role) but have not built the monitor.

## 6. Privacy

Mentions carry phone numbers, order IDs, emails, addresses and images of people. Policy 8
requires redaction before any text leaves the process; `src/llm_classifier.redact()`
implements it for Indian mobile numbers, emails and order IDs, and the UI shows what was
redacted.

**Gaps we know about:** no redaction of names, street addresses, vehicle numbers or faces
in attached images. We store the raw mention text in SQLite unencrypted. Under India's
DPDP Act, public posts are not exempt from purpose limitation simply because they are
public, and a 24-month audit retention (policy 7.3) needs a lawful basis we have not
established.

## 7. Misuse

The same pipeline, pointed slightly differently, becomes:

- **Suppression tooling.** Rank complainants by reach and respond only to the loud ones.
  Our own threshold does exactly this by design — it is the stated purpose — and the
  ethical difference between "triage" and "ignoring powerless customers" is a policy
  choice, not a technical one. A customer with 40 followers and food poisoning is
  currently tiered below a verified account with the same complaint.
- **Critic surveillance.** The author features (prior virality, follower count) are one
  join away from a watchlist of frequent critics.
- **Astroturfing.** The drafter plus the tone guide is a ready-made engine for
  plausible, on-brand replies at volume.

*Mitigation we would insist on:* harm class, never reach, sets the floor for a response
obligation. A SEV-1 health complaint from a 12-follower account must get the same SLA as
one from a verified account. Our severity matrix partially does this (H3 at R0 is still
SEV-2) but reach still moves the tier, and we think that is the most ethically
questionable design decision in the project.

## 8. Model and system failure modes

- **Calibration drift.** The thresholds are probabilities; if the model decalibrates the
  threshold silently means something different. We calibrate with isotonic regression and
  report Brier (0.021), but we have no drift monitor.
- **Temporal leakage.** Avoided by splitting on time, not randomly. If you re-split
  randomly the AUC goes up and the system gets worse.
- **Silent engine fallback.** If the model API fails, the system falls back to the
  offline classifier. It records that it did (`classifier_engine` on every row), but a
  tired approver will not notice the queue is being tiered by keyword matching.
- **Retrieval failure.** Pure TF-IDF retrieved the required playbook clause in only 50%
  of our 8 test cases. We fixed this by pinning the topic's clause and the guardrail
  clauses deterministically, which makes recall 1.0 by construction — but it also means
  retrieval quality is now carried by a hard-coded topic→section map, and a wrong topic
  classification silently retrieves the wrong playbook.

## 9. What we would fix first, in order

1. Real labelled data with inter-annotator agreement — everything else is provisional.
2. A second-model commitment check behind the regex guardrails.
3. Indian-language coverage beyond Hinglish, with per-language slice monitoring.
4. Approver edit-rate monitoring, to detect rubber-stamping.
5. Decouple the response *obligation* from reach, so harm class alone sets the SLA floor.
